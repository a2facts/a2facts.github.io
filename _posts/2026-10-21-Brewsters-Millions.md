# Brewster’s Millions Is a Movie About Solidarity

[This post is also available in video form on YouTube!](https://youtu.be/srgZh2lIGWg)

In 1985, near the peak of his fame, iconic comedian Richard Pryor starred in a movie called _Brewster’s Millions_. The premise: Montgomery Brewster, a minor-league pitcher, inherits a vast fortune from a distant relation. The dying old great-uncle decides he needs to teach Brewster a lesson: spending money is a curse. The fortune is $300 million, but to inherit it, he has to spend $30 million in 30 days, or fail and get nothing at all.

This is not just a wish-fulfillment comedy, and it’s not just a parable about how money can’t buy happiness. The movie is really about solidarity: sticking with your friends, and how the structure of wealth makes that a lot easier for entrenched interests.

I’m going to spoil the hell out of this movie, but it’s 40 years old, so you can’t blame me at this point. Don’t worry, the ending isn’t that big of a surprise.

## The rules

You might think Brewster’s challenge would be easy. But the rules are pretty well thought out to make it hard:

- No accumulating assets. That’s the big one.
- No destroying anything of value.
- No more than 5% gambled away.
- No giving money away, except 5% to charity.

And it seems like an afterthought, but the whole story hinges on this last one:

> You’re not allowed to tell anybody _why_ you have to spend this money. Because I don’t want anybody helpin’ you out! I never had any friends.

Over and over, playing solo is what really makes the challenge hard. And his adversaries are not playing by the same rules.

That’s the point: poor people (and formerly poor people) play by different rules than wealthy people. Poor people are isolated.

That’s what I mean when I say it’s a movie about solidarity. Brewster has a very hard time because he’s an isolated individual. His adversaries have it easy because they’re working together, whether through implicitly aligned interests or outright conspiracy.

## Spending is hard

Brewster buys expensive meals for everyone he meets, hires a fleet of limos, and rents the whole top floor of an expensive hotel. It’s barely a dent in his fortune.

This illustrates a real problem. Some real people in real life have multibillion dollar fortunes. If you have 30 billion dollars and spent it like Brewster, a million dollars a day, you couldn’t spend it in a lifetime (82 years at 0% interest). Brewster was lucky: he only had to spend like crazy for 30 days. If Jeff Bezos or Elon Musk wanted to spend down their fortunes at that rate, it would take centuries. For many billionaires, dividends and interest pay considerably more than $1 million per day. They would _never_ spend it down. They would die richer than they started.

Brewster’s friends try to help, but they make it harder. When they see he’s running out of money, they stage an intervention. They hire him a financial advisor to make money through investments (the last thing he needs!) who gets paid on commission (even worse!).

## Money makes money

Every time he tries to spend, he has to check himself or he accumulates assets. The bank wants to pay him interest; he has to talk them out of it. Longshot bets and bad investments are good ways to lose money, he thinks. But they can pay off. He becomes an accidental venture capitalist. He backs a scheme to haul a giant iceberg to farmers in the desert. Someone even richer buys the company and makes him ten million bucks.

This flies in the face of our unexamined belief in meritocracy. He thought for sure he’d lose because he was making stupid decisions, but as we see again and again in real life, the rich can fail upwards. He spent his whole life thinking money was hard to make. Now that he has to get rid of it, he can’t help but accumulate more.

It’s not that the rich are conspiring. It’s just how capital works. The system, largely set up by the rich, benefits the rich.

Where does real billionaires’ money go? They can’t spend it all, so they buy assets: real estate, gold, stocks, bonds, private companies. One reason everything is so expensive right now (stocks, housing, commodities) is that extremely rich people have a great deal of money and essentially nothing to spend it on. Gary Stevenson talks about this a lot.

This relates to Thomas Piketty’s argument: if a fortune grows faster than the overall economy, it inevitably accumulates, not just in absolute terms but in relative terms.

## People do conspire sometimes

“Conspiracy theory” is a pejorative for an unprovable story. But people do conspire to break the law sometimes.

The rich executors try to pull a fast one. They promise junior lawyer Warren a partnership if Brewster fails in some plausible way. Then they administer the money for a big fee and steer it to their friends. Warren hands Brewster a returned $20,000 furniture deposit at the last minute.
## Solidarity saves him

At the end, we see the solidarity Brewster built. His friends take up a collection so he can start his life over. His buddy, played by John Candy, says he has enough saved that they can just go have fun together. To them, he’s broke, and they stand by him. That’s solidarity.

So how do you spend $20,000 in the 30 seconds before midnight? You can’t! Unless the paralegal accountant, honest to a fault, betrays her employer and blows the whistle. She takes the $20,000 as a retainer to represent him in court. That’s his last dollar, with seconds on the clock.

In the end, it’s loyalty, friendship, and honesty that save Brewster, not just cleverness or determination.

Most stories about solidarity are about kids: ragtag outcasts banding together against Pennywise, the Demogorgon, the Fratelli gang, or Voldemort. Those are all fantasies. It doesn’t have to be a fantasy.

The flip side: there are too many “grindset” stories, where a protagonist trains alone and rises to the top. They seem gritty and practical, a plan anybody could “lock in” and “just do it.”

It’s worth remembering that it’s integrity, friendship, and trust that really make the difference.
